Case Study · Socioknack

Building sales enablement for agents nobody had designed for

Seal Richards had a field-sales operation running on WhatsApp and spreadsheets. We were building Socioknack to replace it, but the first question wasn't "what features?" It was "what are we even replacing?"

🔍 The Problem

The real problem wasn't the tool. It was the feedback loop.

Sales agents were logging visits on WhatsApp, commissions were being calculated by hand, and nobody could tell whether an agent was actually doing the work or just saying they were. Leadership wanted "a sales app." The real problem was trust in field operations.

🚧 The Constraints

Low-trust environment, high-turnover agents, no historical data.

Agents worked in an environment where verified check-ins mattered more than pretty UI. Turnover was high, so any onboarding flow had to work for someone using the app for the very first time, on a low-end Android, on unreliable data. And we had no historical data on how commissions actually got calculated.

⚖️ The Trade-off

Ship a manual approval step, or ship the shiny automation?

The team wanted a full rules engine — commission tiers, bonuses, streaks, penalties, auto-calculated and paid out. I argued the opposite: ship a manual approval step first. Let an admin approve every commission with one tap. It would be slower, uglier, and less "productive." But it would force us to learn how commissions actually got calculated in the messy real world before we automated the wrong rules.

🎯 The Call

Design for the admin, not the agent, in v1.

Most sales apps obsess over the agent's experience. We flipped it: the MVP prioritized the admin dashboard because that's where the trust problem lived. Agents got a minimal check-in flow. The admin got visibility, override, and audit logs.

📈 What Happened

The manual step paid off — not as a feature, as a research instrument.

Within one week of usability testing, we discovered something nobody had documented: commissions weren't one system, they were at least four different systems running at once.

Some agents earned a flat percentage of every sale. Others earned a fixed fee per closed deal, regardless of value. A third group had custom arrangements. And a fourth group was on instalmental payment plans, where the commission itself was split across weeks or months depending on when the customer actually paid.

The manual approval step surfaced all of it and saved us from building an automation engine that would have been wrong.

Case Study · TruthHub

Designing a safety platform where trust is the actual product

TruthHub combined three services under one brand; physical wellness checks ("Knocking"), real-time travel safety ("Check-In"), and digital fact verification. The hard part wasn't building three products. It was making four different user roles feel safe using the same system.

🔍 The Problem

Nigerians lack a single, integrated tool for physical safety AND digital trust.

Existing options were fragmented: private security for physical checks, WhatsApp for family coordination, random fact-check sites for misinformation. No product connected them. The cost wasn't just convenience, it was that families in the diaspora had no reliable way to verify their loved ones back home were okay.

Target users spanned diaspora guardians, elderly living alone, solo travelers, corporate field staff, university students, and NYSC members, each with different needs and different expectations of what "safety" means.

🚧 The Constraints

Four user roles, three services, one MVP launch, with one service deliberately shelved.

The product had to serve Clients, Hosts/Receivers, Agents, and Admins, each with a fundamentally different mental model of the product. On top of that:

Launch timeline: Roughly three months from research to internal QA.

Fact-Checking was planned but couldn't ship in MVP. So it needed to be visible in the UI as "Coming Soon" without confusing users or breaking trust in what was live.

Emergency scenarios; panic buttons, missed check-ins, no-response alerts had to work reliably on day one. In a safety product, failure isn't a bug; it's a real-world injury.

⚖️ The Trade-off

Build one unified experience across three services or keep them separate?

The obvious approach was to build three mini-products, each with its own flow. It would ship faster and each team could work independently.

But that would break the core promise: a diaspora guardian shouldn't have to learn three different mental models. If Knocking lived here and Check-In lived there, families would always be guessing where to look during an emergency.

The trade-off was that a unified experience meant more design work, more shared components, and more coordination with the same three-month window.

🎯 The Call

Unify the shell, differentiate the services, ship Fact-Checking as "Coming Soon."

I structured TruthHub as one navigation, three clearly-labeled services. The app's main menu shows all three products:Knocking, Check-In, and Fact-Checking, with the inactive one visibly staged as "Coming Soon" rather than hidden.

This did three things at once: it kept users oriented (one app, one mental model), it built anticipation for the future service, and it let us ship MVP without compromising the brand story we were promising.

Internally, the MVP prioritized the two services where failure has the highest human cost: Knocking (physical visits to loved ones) and Check-In (real-time travel safety). Fact-Checking was deferred to V2 with a clear roadmap.

🧩 What Shaped The Design

Four user roles meant four different definitions of "safe."

Clients (diaspora guardians, parents, travelers) needed visibility and control - they had to know their loved ones were safe, see the history of visits, and manage reports, alerts, scheduling, wallet.

Hosts/Receivers (elderly users, students, NYSC members) needed dignity and predictability. They had to know when someone was coming, verify the agent's identity before opening the door, and not feel surveilled.

Agents (field staff) needed efficiency and protection. Clear assignments, offline-capable reporting, and a way to escalate when something felt wrong on their end.

Admins needed oversight without chaos. A dashboard to assign, reschedule, respond to alerts, and audit reports, all in real time.

The feature set wasn't designed around services. It was designed around making each of these four roles feel in control, sometimes with completely different UI in the same app.

Case Study · Sportverse

When the business model gets blocked, redesign the frame not the feature list

Sportverse was pitched as a virtual sports betting app. Mid-sprint, regulatory risk around real-money betting killed the business model. We limited time the team needed a decision.

🔍 The Problem

The value proposition was legal exposure.

"Virtual sports betting" sounds clever until you realize regulators read that as "unlicensed betting." The launch-blocking risk wasn't technical it was that the entire pitch required a licensing regime we didn't have.

🚧 The Constraints

Limited tine, pre-regulatory environment, and an engineering team ready to build.

Pausing the product meant losing the team. Continuing as-is meant launching something that might get shut down. We had to find the version of this product that could ship.

⚖️ The Trade-off

Delay launch for a betting license, or reframe the entire product?

Licensing could take 12+ months. Delaying meant losing momentum, team, and patience. Reframing meant throwing out the pitch deck but keeping the engine.

🎯 The Call

Keep the betting engine. Swap the money for status.

The core mechanics (odds, prediction, streaks) had nothing to do with cash. We replaced currency with points, badges, leaderboards and bragging rights. Fantasy leagues, trivia, prizes, all preserved. Real-money betting completely removed. Same engine, new frame.

📈 What Happened

Shipped MVP, zero regulatory exposure, and a fundamentally more loyal user base.

The non-monetary version attracted users who'd never touch a betting app, casual sports fans who wanted to prove they knew more than their friends. A different audience than we'd planned for and arguably a better one.

Case Study · HerTechTrail

Three problems, one product team: live cohorts, self-paced learning, and a live event

At HerTechTrail I worked on three different things. The live 12-week PM cohorts, a self-paced LMS for learners who couldn't join live classes, and the TechHerFest24 event website. Each one had a different audience and a different problem. Here's how I approached each of them.

🔍 The Problem

Learners were leaving before they finished, and a lot of people never joined at all.

Two separate problems showed up around the same time:

1. Learners dropped off in live cohorts. In the 12-week PM program, learners made it to week 6 or 7, then disappeared. They had already invested six weeks. Something in the middle of the program was breaking them.

2. Many learners couldn't join live classes at all. Some had jobs that didn't allow them to sit through scheduled evening classes. Some lived in different time zones. Some just needed more flexibility. The live cohort model worked for most people, but not for everyone and we had no product for the people it didn't work for.

🚧 The Constraints

Live classes were already running. Small team. No budget for a full rebuild.

Every class was live and recorded. Instructors taught in real time. Learners had already committed to that schedule. We couldn't stop the program to redesign it.

On the LMS side, my role was to work with the team on review, ideation, and testing not to build the platform myself. I helped shape what got built and how we checked whether it worked.

And separately, the TechHerFest24 event needed its own website built from scratch. I owned that end to end.

⚖️ The Trade-offs

Three problems. Three different decisions about what to fix.

On the TechHerFest24 site: The easy path was to build a simple event page with dates and a ticket button. But the event was about community and energy. A plain page would not sell tickets. I pushed for a page that felt like the event itself.

I managed the full build of the event website, from idea to launch. On the About section, I ran an A/B test: a video of the previous year's event against a still image. The video won, and it brought more ticket sales. Moving images of a real event communicated energy and community in a way that a photo could not.

📈 What Happened

Engagement up 60%. Event registrations up 40%.

The changes to the live cohort helped learners get past week 6 and 7. Across the academy, learner engagement went up 60%.

TechHerFest24 registrations went up 40%, and we launched the event website 3 weeks earlier than planned.

Case Study · Eko Crib

Trust is a feature but only after you know what people don't trust

Eko Crib was our attempt to fix house-hunting in Lagos. The obvious problem was "too many fake listings." The honest problem was "we didn't yet know which kind of fake mattered most to users."

🔍 The Problem

House hunters in Lagos weren't struggling to find listings. They were struggling to trust them.

Dozens of real-estate sites existed. Users had stopped using them because listings were often duplicates, already-rented, or straight-up fake. The real problem wasn't supply. It was signal.

🚧 The Constraints

No verification team. No budget for on-the-ground checks.

A "verified listing" feature required physical inspection or a trusted partner network. We had neither.

⚖️ The Trade-off

Ship a weak verification system, or ship no verification and instrument the problem?

Weak verification is worse than no verification. It erodes trust twice (once when the listing is fake, twice when the "verified" badge turns out to be meaningless). I chose the harder path: ship without verification, and add a one-tap "Report this listing" button. Ugly, but honest.

How I Decide

The judgment behind the work.

These aren't mantras. They're decisions I've actually had to make and the reasoning behind them.

01

Start with the problem.

Not the feature.

Eko Crib: Users didn't want more listings they wanted to trust the ones they saw. That reframed the whole roadmap.

02

Make complexity clear.

Good product work creates clarity.

TruthHub: "Track everything" became "prove visits happened." Simpler, cheaper, more honest.

03

Build the smallest useful thing.

MVP doesn't mean minimum effort.

Socioknack: A manual approval step beat a beautiful rules engine because it taught us the real rules.

04

Talk to users.

Assumptions are expensive.

Teket: 20 organizer conversations changed what "discovery" meant. Would have guessed wrong without them.

05

Ship. Learn. Improve.

A product isn't finished when it launches.

Sportverse: Reframed the entire product around non-monetary play. Same engine, better audience.
